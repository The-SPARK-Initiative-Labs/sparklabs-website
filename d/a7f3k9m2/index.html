Ben Farris

The whole thing, written down

PART ONE — WHERE I CAME FROM

The pen

I was born to a fifteen-year-old girl named Susan.

Her boyfriend was a man named Curtis who had schizophrenia. What I know about that time is what I've been told and what my body remembers. They kept me outside in a pen and would go out and party. At night the roaches got on me — crawled over me while I was in bed or wherever they left me — and I have been terrified of roaches my entire life for a reason I did not choose and never had a say in. Curtis would punch me in the abdomen to get me to stop crying. I have photos of myself covered in bug bites from head to toe, from being left alone outside in this pen.

I was eighteen months old.

That's when my grandparents came and got me, and adopted me, and became my mother and father. I have never called them anything else, because that is what they were. Everything else in this document happens downstream of that decision.

Susan tells it differently. She says it was supposed to be temporary. She says a lot of things. She was in and out of mental institutions her whole childhood with some kind of impulse control problem, and I have spent forty years watching her rewrite things that happened. I don't believe her, and I've stopped needing to argue about it.

My father

Ben Farris. Born February 21, 1935.

Eighteen years in the Navy. A welder. He made chief. Then he came home and welded another twenty years for the LCRA. He smoked three packs a day starting when he was around twelve years old, and by the end of his life he told me breathing felt like breathing through a coffee straw.

I was attached to that man at the hip. We did everything together, every day, for as long as he was alive. If you want to understand anything about me, understand that: I was raised by a man born in 1935 who took me everywhere with him and did not put me down.

My mother

Dorothy Farris. Born May 8, 1941. A registered nurse for her entire working life.

She never once told me she loved me. Not one time, in her life or mine.

When I was old enough to ask my father why he said it constantly and she never said it at all, he told me the truth: he had wanted to adopt me and she had not. I could tell it was true. She wasn't cruel to me. She just wasn't warm, and I knew the difference by the time I was small.

Stevie

Before me, they had a son. His name was Stevie and he died at eighteen months old.

He was born with the plates of his skull grown together too early, and with webbed hands and feet. Nobody ever gave my parents a name for it. They blamed the hospital — he'd had a surgery, and afterward they wouldn't let my mother be with him, and she believed until the day she died that being kept from him is what killed him.

Stevie died at eighteen months. I came to them at eighteen months.

Everybody in my family knows what that means. So do I. I was a replacement. A grown woman had lost a baby, and her unstable daughter had a baby she couldn't keep, and the math was'nt complicated.

I don't say that bitterly. They saved my life. But I've never been able to un-know it either.

Why I've never fit anywhere

I was raised by people born in 1935 and 1941.

That is not one generation off from the people my age. It's two. I was raised on their music, their manners, their sense of how a man is supposed to conduct himself, their politics, their assumption that you don't complain and you don't ask. Every room I've ever walked into as an adult has felt like a room I arrived at late.

People say "you seem like you're from a different time or something" I am. It's not a personality. It's math.

The piano

My mother played hymns. That's what started it. At 2 years old, I was reaching up to keys I couldn't see, trying to play like her. So, at four, my father put me in piano lessons with Mrs. Grable. She lived by the elementary school campus here in town.

By the time I was six years old my piano teacher told my father that I was playing better than she was. I'm not saying that to brag — I've got very little to brag about. I'm saying it because it's the one thing I was given that was unambiguously mine. I didn't work for it. It was just there.

I played hymns for my mother all the time.

Years later, at the end, when the Alzheimer's had taken her speech and she no longer knew who I was, I would sit down and play the same hymns. And she would lean her head back, and close her eyes, and smile.

I don't know whether she knew it was me. I know she knew what she was hearing.

I have since been told that musical memory is one of the very last things that that disease takes — that people who can't name their own children still respond to songs they've known for fifty years. So it probably was exactly what it looked like. She couldn't reach me. She could still reach the music. And the music had me in it.

I taught my daughter on those same keys. I showed her the pattern in the black keys — two, then three, then two, then three — and where an A is and how it runs up to G and starts over again. She had it immediately. Same ear. Three generations of this family now, have been playing the same keys.

PART TWO — TWENTY-SIX YEARS OF TRYING TO WORK

Reba's Pizza

I got my first job at fourteen, at Reba's Pizza, here in La Grange.

I am forty now. That's twenty-six years.

In twenty-six years I have never held a job longer than about four months. Most of them lasted two or three weeks.

I want to be precise about the pattern, because the pattern is the whole thing. It was never that I got bored, or got fired for attitude, or quit because I didn't like it. Every single one ended the same way: my blood sugar, my blood pressure, my eyes, my legs. I could not stay on my feet. I would last a few days or a few weeks and then my body would take the decision away from me.

That has happened, in one form or another, for twenty-six years, at every kind of job a man can get in a town this size.

The last one

Towers Nursing Home, Smithville, Texas. November 2025 through February 2026.

I want to lay this one out in detail because it is the clearest picture of what happens to me.

I had a vitrectomy — eye surgery — one day before my first shift.

I went to work anyway. I worked my first three days with a patch over my eye, because I needed that job and I was not going to lose it before I started it.

They put me on as a hospitality aide while I waited for the nurse aide class to open, so I could get recertified as a CNA and move up. My legs started going in the first week. Not soreness. The thing my legs do. Diabetic neuropathy. Fluid in my joints. Edema.

Then the class started, and I missed three days.

One for my legs. One for my blood sugar. One because my eye surgeon called me in to tell me the surgery had left me with permanent damage to my vision.

Three absences gets you removed from the class. That's how I lost the job.

I did not quit. I got taken out of it by my own body, one medical appointment at a time. Every one of those three days has a record with a date on it, held by a doctor, and anyone who wants to check is welcome to.

That was the longest job I have ever held. Four months.

PART THREE — SEVEN YEARS I WORKED FOR FREE

What happened to my father

Before he died, my father was helping a group of men lower a septic tank into a hole they had just dug with a rented backhoe.

He was leaned over the top edge of the tank, adjusting the chains. The man running the machine (my stepfather, Susan's husband) stepped off it and caught the lever that drops the bucket.

It nearly cut my father in half.

I was at a waterpark that day with my wife (girlfriend at the time) and her mother. Her mother got a phone call in the front seat of the car. I was in the back. My first words, before anybody said anything to me and before I even knew what he'd been doing that day, or where he was, were "something happened to my dad." I could feel it. I don't know how, but I just knew.

When I got there, there were beer cans on the machine and on the ground and everywhere you looked. Every man on that job was drunk.

My father didn't drink. Ever. Not a day in his life. He hated alcohol.

It shattered his hip — they had to fuse it, and he never walked right again. It pushed his stomach up into his heart hard enough to bruise the heart. They also fused a few of his vertebrae, because it broke his back too. They took out half his diaphragm. This is a man who already had COPD and emphysema and was already breathing through that coffee straw.

He did not die on that job site. But the math was finished that day.

What I did about it

I was a certified nurse's aide. The certification has since lapsed, because I couldn't keep a job to keep it current.

He started to decline. Alzheimer's. For about three years I bathed my father, changed him, and fed him. Every day. Until he died in 2014.

Then my mother

About eight months after he died, we found my mother wandering out in the twenty-nine acres of woods behind the house.

It was Alzheimer's. Again.

She went to Jefferson Place, the assisted living facility here in town. Then to Monument Hill, and Care Inn. Then she died there. The whole process took six to eight years — I can't give an exact year for her death, and I feel bad about that, but that disease doesn't hand you a date. It takes the person out in pieces over half a decade and there's no single moment for your mind to hold onto.

My wife helped with her at home, until we couldn't take care of her anymore. My biological mother and her brother would occasionally show up and "perform" care. I was the one there at three in the morning…

What that adds up to

Seven years. No pay. No employer. No title. No résumé line.

I know it doesn't count as employment. Nobody can call a company and verify it.

But when somebody in this town tells you that Ben Farris doesn't want to work, that is what I was doing for seven straight years, unpaid, for two dying people, with my own hands.

PART FOUR — MY BODY

I am going to lay this out plainly, because it's the load-bearing claim in everything I'm asking for, and because I have spent my whole life having people not believe it.

What I have

Type 1 diabetes. I use a Tandem Mobi insulin pump and a Dexcom G7 continuous glucose monitor. The insulin has to stay refrigerated. Remember that — it matters later.

Stage 3B chronic kidney disease with nephrotic-range protein loss. Found in July of 2026. It was in my lab results. Nobody flagged it, nobody called me, and no follow-up was scheduled.

Proliferative diabetic retinopathy. This is the advanced kind. I had a vitrectomy in November 2025 and I have a permanent tilt in my vision — a degree or two, forever. It's never going away.

Diabetic neuropathy. My legs have burned, on and off, since I was about twenty years old. Twenty years.

Something in my head that nobody has ever named. When I bend my head down about forty-five degrees — and only when I'm standing up — I get pressure in my head and my vision starts to go. If I kept doing it, I'd pass out. Sitting down, it doesn't happen. Standing, it does, every time. No doctor has ever told me what this is.

Insomnia. Since I was adopted. My parents said I never slept right as a baby and I never have since. I don't sleep at night. That's not a complaint, it's just the fact.

What a day actually looks like

I get out of bed. I walk about sixty yards to a portable building on my property where our computers are. I sit at a desk. I stay there until I go to bed, which is always very late.

The only real walking I do all week is at the grocery store.

Here is the part I need people to understand: most days, nothing hurts.

That is not because I'm fine. It's because I have built a life with no standing in it. I'm not managing symptoms — I'm avoiding every single activity that produces them. I've shrunk my life down to precisely the radius my body will allow, and inside that radius I look okay.

That's why people see me at a computer and think there's nothing wrong. They don't see that the chair is the accommodation.

The last time I tried anyway

The last physical thing I did was play soccer in the yard with my eight-year-old son.

That's what caused the bleed in my eye that led to the surgery that left permanent damage. November 2025.

He still asks me to play. He forgets. And I have to tell him no, and then he remembers, and he gets sad, and we do it again a week later.

That is the actual cost, in a form anyone can picture.

What it's been like getting care

I want to be careful here. I'm not writing this to attack anyone. But if someone is deciding whether to trust me, they should know why I don't have a specialist and why my kidney disease sat unaddressed.

My primary care doctor prescribed me prescription-strength ibuprofen while I was on two other medications and had Stage 3B kidney disease — a combination that can shut a kidney down. He delayed urgent eye referrals twice, taking a month when it should have been a day, and I have permanent vision damage as a result. My July 2026 labs showed nephrotic-range protein and nobody flagged it or scheduled anything.

The endocrinologist I was referred to saw me exactly one time, set me up with the pump, never followed up, and refused to rebook me, and just disappeared.

So when somebody asks why I don't just go see a doctor about all this — that's why. It isn't stubbornness. It's twenty-six years of experience, and no other doctors to see.

PART FIVE — THE MARRIAGE

Twenty-two years

I met Amy when I was fourteen years old — the same year I started at Reba's. We married at seventeen or eighteen. She is, functionally, my entire adult life. We divorced when I was around 37 or so.

Linken

When we were fourteen we planned that one day we'd get married and have a son and his name would be Linken. That was the plan from the time we were children.

She took birth control until she was twenty-five without telling me. Eight years of trying to have a child while secretly on birth control.

Our daughter Julia was born when we where twenty-five. Linken came eight years after that, by accident.

The two children I have, I have in spite of that, not because of it.

What kind of father I was allowed to be

She would not let me parent them. I was only a weapon she deployed against them, because she didn't want to be "the bad guy".

She gave them their baths — and kept doing it well past the age when a kid should be washing themselves. I wanted to teach them how. She wouldn't have it. If I bathed them, she bathed them again afterward, because it "wasn't good enough".

They slept in our bed. Julia until she was about seven. Linken until he was about five. We had bought cribs. They had their own rooms, furnished and ready.

I slept on the couch in my own house for those years.

How it ended

There was no affair. We were together twenty-four hours a day; there wasn't room for one. I never hit her. We rarely argued. I want that on the record because in a town this size people assume.

One day she asked if I needed anything from the grocery store. I said no. She left with the kids and did not come back.

The reason — the actual reason — is that she was afraid I wouldn't be able to handle the property taxes on my mother's house and land.

I begged her to stay for the children. I told her we could live at opposite ends of the house and never speak. It made no difference to her.

PART SIX — HOW MY CHILDREN CAME TO LIVE WITH ME

This part matters more than the divorce does, and I want it in writing, in order.

Her mother's house

Amy moved back into her mother's house — the house she herself had lived in from childhood until the day she left to marry me.

That house was not safe for my children.

It was/is in disrepair. There's a German Shepherd there, a big one, completely uncontrolled, that goes out of its mind in storms and rain. Both of my kids are terrified of it. There is feces and urine soaked through the whole house. No AC or heat at times. It's a problem.

There is also a woman living in that house who has been badly mistreated for years — and Amy has talked about reporting it, out loud, to me, repeatedly, for years, and has never once done it. I have listened to her say she was going to for longer than I can count.

What I was about to do

I was about to call the police.

And Amy knew it.

She agreed with me that the house wasn't safe for them. That is when my children came to live with me, in hotels around town while I waited for my house and land to be ready for us, and that is the actual origin of this arrangement. Not me taking them. Not her walking out on them. Her conceding that where she was living was not fit for them, and handing them to me. From that day, she never acted like their mother again.

She told me she would take Linken back when her own house was finished.

We both agreed Julia would stay with me permanently. And she later allowed her daughter, at 14, to disown her. They don't talk to each other at all anymore.

None of it was ever put in writing. That is a problem I am now trying to fix, and it is the reason I have no legal standing on paper for a situation that has been true for years.

What's happened since

Her house has been finished for about a year.

Linken has been there exactly one time. He has never spent a single night in it.

That is not me withholding him. She lives less than a mile from my front door. She can be here in three minutes.

In all of 2026, she has bought Linken one pair of shoes and three pairs of shorts. That is the complete and entire list of what she has provided her children this year. She has never given me a dollar.

She still claims both children on her taxes.

She comes over and visits — and I want to describe what that actually looks like, because it's the part nobody outside this house sees. She sits down across from me and talks to me for an hour, about her life, like I'm a friend, while both of her children sit ten feet away at their computers and are not spoken to. They prefer it that way. Every time the phone rings or her car pulls up, they groan and complain.

PART SEVEN — MY CHILDREN

Julia, sixteen

She is the most beautiful, kind, funny person I have ever known. That's not a father talking. That's just true.

Left alone with nothing asked of her, she draws. She journals. She makes things pretty — that's her whole orientation toward the world. She makes TikTok dances she is not allowed to post, because nobody in this house is on social media until they're eighteen, purely so she can watch them, laugh at them, and show them to me. There are hundreds of them in a shared album on my phone right now.

She's sixteen years old and she still makes things to show her dad.

She plays piano. She has the same ear I do.

Linken, eight

He reads constantly. Mostly about science. That is exactly, precisely what I was doing at eight years old.

He draws. He builds things out of Legos. He goes outside and dorks around in the backyard. He gets on his sister's nerves professionally.

There are time limits on games and YouTube in this house, and they are enforced.

What I know about myself as a father

I'm not going to be modest here.

I am an outstanding father. Nobody who has actually seen me with my children will deny it.

At the rare family gathering I get invited to, people look irritated — actually irritated — because my kids are the best-behaved children in the room. They can't square it with the version of me they've decided on, so they look away from it.

I homeschool them both, by myself, every day.

What they know

My children know exactly how bad this is. Both of them. They know why the grocery cart is thin. They know what's happening.

I tell them it's going to work out, and they believe me. A sixteen-year-old and an eight-year-old are watching their father run out of money and choosing to trust him anyway.

They still eat. I want to be honest about that because it matters: they graze all day — sandwiches, snacks, fries, something to hold them until dinner. They do not go hungry.

I'm the one who eats once a day.

That's the arrangement I chose and I'd choose it again. It is also not sustainable, and I know it.

PART EIGHT — THE MONEY

What I had

When my mother died, her house and the land sold, and I ended up with about half a million dollars.

What I did with it

I kept two acres of the property and put a trailer house on it. That's where we live now. It's paid for.

I bought a truck. I bought a boat. I furnished the house. I furnished the kids' rooms. Beds, clothes, toys, TVs, computers — everything three people need to start a life from zero, which is exactly what we were doing.

And some of it I flat wasted. I'm not going to dress that up or make excuses.

Here's the one thing I'll say about it, because it explains more than "he was irresponsible" does: nobody ever taught me anything about money. Or taxes. Or titles. Or how any of the machinery of the world works. My parents loved me and they got that part wrong, deliberately — they'd disciplined the two children before me hard and watched them turn out badly, so with me they tried the opposite. And I came out the other side of it as a grown man who did not know how a car title worked.

I've paid for that gap over and over. I'm still paying for it.

What I sold, in order

All of this is on bank statements.

September 2025 — I sold my older truck to Carvana for $5,800.

February 16, 2026 — I traded my 2024 Ford F-150, which I owned outright, at Group 1 Ford of South Austin. The trade allowance was $31,000. It came back as a used 2019 Subaru Impreza with 130,000 miles on it and $15,113.27 in cash.

I lost my job in February. I traded the truck fourteen days later.

The boat — I bought it for around $2800. I sold it for $2,000 to my brother-in-law, who lives a hundred yards from my house and who bought my mother's place. It was the one thing the three of us genuinely loved doing together. He was supposed to pay me $100 a month. He's paid once in three months. He walked me through registering it in my name so I could sign the title over to him, and then stopped explaining things to me at exactly the point where explaining things stopped being in his interest.

What I own now

Two acres, paid for, with a trailer house on it.

A used Subaru.

Computers my kids do school on and that I'd need for any job I could physically hold.

That's the whole list. Everything else has already been converted to cash and spent on staying alive.

PART ELEVEN — WHAT I HAVE ALREADY TRIED

I want this written down so that nobody has to wonder whether I've looked, and so nobody has to spend their time suggesting something I did years ago.

Disability. About twenty filings across my life. Several attorneys. Denied every time. The most recent one never even produced a decision — it simply disappeared. And even if I filed again tomorrow and won, it takes over a year. I do not have a year.

SNAP. Denied.

Medicaid. Denied. Texas didn't expand it. I fall in the coverage gap — too poor to qualify for help, and not in the right category to qualify for the other kind.

Free legal help. Every legal aid office and free attorney I could locate, including the regional one that covers this county. They don't take cases like mine.

Work I could physically do. I have applied for remote and sit-down work more times than I can count. Every posting has a requirement I can't meet — a degree, a work history, a certification. My résumé is twenty-six years of jobs that lasted three weeks.

Selling everything. Done. The truck, the other truck, the boat, and every possession that wasn't load-bearing.

Cutting everything. Done. $550 a month, documented above.

My family. There are about twenty adults in my extended family. Most of them attend churches in this town. Not one of them will help me.

The reason, as best I understand it, is that they believe I am lying about how sick I am. They see a man sitting at a computer. That's the whole basis for it.

Asking. I have asked.

I got down on my knees in front of my biological mother — the woman who left me in that pen — and cried, and asked her for help. She told me I needed to look harder. Try harder. Apply for more jobs I can't do. She had been sending me job listings for work my body cannot perform, and when I finally broke down and asked her directly, that was the answer.

In twenty-six years, no one has ever said yes.

I'm not writing that so anyone will feel bad. I'm writing it because it explains something: every single person I've ever asked already had a fixed opinion about me before I opened my mouth. My family decided who I was decades ago. Susan decided. The town decided.

A pastor, or a group of people in a church, is the first place in my life where the answer isn't already decided before I ask.

PART TWELVE — WHAT I BELIEVE

I was raised in the church. I was devout until I was thirteen years old. I know the Bible front to back — not casually, not as a cultural thing. I read it and I believed it.

At thirteen, logic kicked in. I started questioning things I'd accepted without question for thriteen years, and they stopped holding together for me. That wasn't rebellion. It happened because I took it seriously enough to actually examine it.

Then I spent some years being one of those loud, obnoxious atheists. And I grew out of that too — because at some point I asked myself why I hated religious people, and the honest answer was that I didn't. I was just carrying around a different ideology and mistaking it for thinking.

Where I've landed is this: I think something made all of this. And if something made it, then that thing is God. I'm not sure what else to call it.

I would not want to live in any society other than a Judeo-Christian one. Whatever else the Bible is, it is a guide for a decent way to live. I am raising my children conservative, with those values, on purpose.

I may not believe in God. I'm not entirely sure. But I try to live like I do.

I could have walked into a church office and said I was a believer. It would have been easier and it would have gone over better. I'm not going to lie to a pastor to get money. If I'm asking people to trust what I say about my kidneys and my kids and my bank account, I'm not going to start by lying about the one thing that's easiest to lie about.

PART THIRTEEN — WHAT I'M ASKING FOR

$1,340 a month. That is $310 a week.

Sixteen people giving twenty dollars a week. Or thirty-one people at ten dollars.

Sixteen people is a small group. It's a Sunday school class. It's a men's Bible study. It's one home group that meets on a Wednesday night.

I would rather it be many people giving a little than one person carrying us, for two reasons. If it's spread out, nobody is hurt when it stops. And if it's spread out, nobody is carrying my family alone.

How long. Until something changes. I'm being straight about that because I won't pretend there's a date. What could change: I get a court order that makes my children's mother pay support. I find work I can physically do. My health situation changes what I'm eligible for. Any of those would reduce or end what I need. I'm actively working all three.

Where it goes. Anyone who wants to pay a bill directly instead of handing me money should do exactly that. Pay Fayette Electric. Pay the water co-op. I would rather it work that way. There is no version of this where anyone has to take my word about where a dollar went.

What I'll give back. I can't offer money. What I can offer is this: I will answer any question anyone has, in front of anybody, at any time. I'll stand up in front of the congregation and say all of it out loud and take questions. I play piano — I'll play for you every Sunday for the rest of my life if that's useful to anybody. And I'll show you my bank statements every single month, unprompted, so nobody ever has to wonder.

THE LAST THING
